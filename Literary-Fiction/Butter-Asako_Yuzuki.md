# Butter - Asako Yuzuki, Polly Barton (Translator)

## Summary
- Rika visits Reiko, her new marital house, after giving up her work. Rika is a journalist. Reiko gives up her job because the obstetrician said maybe stress caused her to have difficulty conceiving after two years of marriage. Reiko's husband is Ryosuke. Reiko and Rika have been friends since uni.
- Manako Kajii is a convicted suspect who uses a dating service to extort money from a succession of men who are looking to get married. She is also accused of killing three of them. She often updated her blog with extravagant food and other luxuries and spent her time going to restaurants, ordering special dishes, and proud of her abilities to cook. The news is sensational because Manako weighs over 70 kg and is neither young nor beautiful. She went to a prestigious French cooking school to learn her cooking and is picky about butter.
- Manako refuses to meet any journalists, especially female journalists. However, after Rika followed Reiko's advice by requesting Manako for the beef stew recipe, Manako replied to Rika's letter and was willing to meet her in the detention house. Manako agrees to meet Rika because she's looking for someone to talk about delicious food. Manako despises feminists and margarine. She suggests Rika try to enjoy a bowl of rice with a specific brand of butter and soy sauce. Rika, who doesn't have a rice cooker at home and never cooks in her kitchen, decides to try the recipe as part of her research and is also hoping that Manako will meet her again. Rika never expects such a simple dish will change her perception about food.
- Manako still refuses to talk about the case. Rika offers herself as a tool that can eat things, feel things, and see things on behalf of Manako so that Manako can feel free in a spiritual way.

<br>

## Personal Thoughts
* It's a story about a journalist, Rika, who is eager to cover the news of Manako Kajii, a woman serial killer, and becomes obsessed with the food suggested by Manako. Throughout the story, there are discussions about gender, misogyny, and societal expectations about women in Japan. There is a certain standard to perceive what is acceptable and what is unforgivable about how women choose their lives, which is non-existent for men.
* I agreed with some of the things mentioned in the book regarding how society treats women differently than men. At the same time, some of the viewpoints about men seem quite extreme and a bit nitpicking. It felt as if the writer went all out to pick the bones out of something.
* I swallowed my saliva every time it mentioned the word 'butter'. The description for each food is so mouthwatering, and the beautiful vocabulary just to highlight the simple ingredients such as butter makes it so tempting to taste it in real life.
* If I put aside the discussion of gender disparity and focus on the story itself, I feel it's an intriguing read, like the butter with the white rice mixed with soy sauce. If the feminism theme is in moderation, this book would be a great read. Unfortunately, as the story progresses, the elements of feminism are getting overwhelming. There was a part in the story where a dairy farmer expresses his general opinion and how the villagers view Manako, but the opinions are being perceived as misogynistic. When I reached the middle of the story, I felt it was leaning towards delivering a message. I don't know whether I was lost with the story's direction or the writer was attempting to portray Rika being sucked into Manako's world.
